[01]Problem Space & Motivation
背景与动机 · 现实脱节

The Cultural Disconnect at Maple Bridge

Exploring the gap between rich historical heritage and on-site visitor interpretation.

The Context

A thousand-year landmark experienced as a fleeting photo stop.

“Visitors come to Maple Bridge to admire its crescent stone arch, but often depart without understanding why a Tang poet wept here in 756 AD.”

Maple Bridge (枫桥, Fengqiao) in Suzhou is an internationally revered heritage site shaped by classical Tang poetry, ancient Grand Canal waterways, and Jiangnan architectural aesthetics (Suzhou Municipal People’s Government, 2021). However, field research and user interviews reveal that the contemporary on-site experience is predominantly visual and surface-level.

Visitors arrive seeking cultural depth, yet encounter fragmented physical plaques, language barriers, and crowded paths. To understand what they are looking at, visitors are forced to constantly look down at mobile screens—navigating translation tools, encyclopedia pages, and commercial maps. This creates a sharp experiential paradox: the more digital effort visitors invest to understand the place, the further removed they become from its serene atmosphere.

This case study documents our HCI design process: investigating this cultural interpretation gap, synthesizing user needs, and creating Maple Bridge—an audio-first, context-aware cultural companion that restores environmental immersion.

5Key Insights
In-SituGrand Canal
Insight 01

Understanding is Blocked by Language and Context

Visitors frequently admire Maple Bridge visually but cannot decipher its historical significance because on-site plaques are primarily in Chinese, fragmented, or inaccessible to non-native visitors. This creates an interpretation disconnect where visitors see the architecture without grasping its cultural resonance (Casillo et al., 2020).

Participant Observation & Quote

“Participants noted they “knew nothing” about the background beforehand and struggled because physical signage offered little English explanation.”

Design ImplicationProvide bilingual contextual narration (English & Mandarin) directly keyed to landmarks, eliminating reliance on clunky external translation apps.
Directly addresses Glinda Cottrill (Curious Cultural Explorer)
Insight 02

Hands-Free Audio Interaction Supports Immersion

Participants consistently favored listening over reading during their visit. Staring down at phone screens disrupted the contemplative atmosphere of the water-town, whereas lightweight audio allowed them to keep their eyes on the canal, stone bridges, and foliage (Xue et al., 2025).

Participant Observation & Quote

“Participants repeatedly asked for voiceover or audio guides so they could “just enjoy the scenery” rather than constantly checking their phones.”

Design ImplicationPrioritize location-aware audio storytelling as the primary sensory modality, relegating screen interactions to quiet confirmation cues.
Benefits both Glinda Cottrill and Li Mei
Insight 03

Pre-Visit Awareness Reduces Uncertainty and Stress

Visitors experience anxiety regarding crowding, visiting windows, and spatial layout before they set foot on the grounds. Having clear foresight into crowd patterns and landmark previews allows them to arrive feeling composed and in control (Zhang et al., 2025).

Participant Observation & Quote

“Users described checking crowd forecasts and wanting preview maps of routes and landmarks before traveling to the site.”

Design ImplicationIntroduce a pre-visit planning layer featuring structured crowd forecasts (Today, This Week, By Spot) and landmark previews.
Directly addresses Li Mei (Atmosphere-First Visitor)
Insight 04

Excessive Interaction Creates Cognitive Friction

Frequent phone interactions, switching between translation utilities, maps, and camera apps fragments the visit and creates cognitive overload. Rather than feeling like a serene cultural retreat, the visit begins to feel like a high-friction administrative task.

Participant Observation & Quote

“Interview responses highlighted frustration with having to “constantly look down at the phone” to piece together fragmented bits of history.”

Design ImplicationDesign an ultra-minimal visual interface with gentle spatial cues, preventing app switching and visual clutter while walking.
Reduces cognitive load across all visitor profiles
Insight 05

Engagement Should Be Optional, Not Forced

While gamified mechanics and quizzes appeal to some users, others actively reject them as gimmicky and disrespectful to heritage calm. Cultural technology must balance playful curiosity with deep respect for quiet contemplation (Peng et al., 2023; Pasca et al., 2021).

Participant Observation & Quote

“Reactions were strongly mixed: some participants welcomed playful quests, while others insisted that aggressive badges and gamification feel out of place.”

Design ImplicationKeep deep interaction, chatbot Q&A, and collectible photo spots strictly opt-in, preserving a tranquil default experience.
Respects Li Mei’s quiet preferences while enabling Glinda’s curiosity

Synthesized Insights

Strategic Insights Guiding Architectural Choices

01

Visitors want cultural meaning without losing immersion

Many participants felt Maple Bridge was visually beautiful but difficult to fully understand, especially when historical context was sparse, fragmented, or only available in Chinese. The core need was not just more information, but access to meaning without interrupting the experience. This aligns with research on cultural heritage chatbots, where context-aware systems deliver information dynamically without requiring explicit user effort (Casillo et al., 2020; Sperlì, 2021).

Derived: R1, R4, finding-lang, finding-screenLeads to: DG1, DG2, decision-3
02

Audio is preferred on-site because it keeps attention on the place

Participants consistently preferred audio or heads-up guidance while walking, since reading from the phone pulled them away from the environment. This supports prior findings that immersive and responsive interaction modalities improve user engagement in cultural tourism contexts (Xue et al., 2025).

Derived: R1, finding-audioLeads to: DG1, Decision-2
03

Planning support matters before the visit begins

Crowd awareness, landmark previews, and timing information were especially valuable before arrival. Visitors wanted to make decisions in advance so they could avoid uncertainty, choose calmer times, and enter the site with a better sense of direction (Zhang et al., 2025).

Derived: R2, finding-planning, persona-2Leads to: DG3, decision-1
04

Excessive phone interaction creates cognitive and experiential friction

Constantly looking down at mobile screens to piece together fragmented translations and map pins disrupts the atmosphere and turns what should be a peaceful cultural visit into a fragmented, task-heavy chore. Interaction must stay in the background.

Derived: R4, finding-screenLeads to: DG2, decision-3
05

Deeper interaction should be optional, not the default

Interactive stories, collectibles, and AI-based features appealed to some visitors, but others found them distracting, childish, or unnecessary. This suggested that engagement features should deepen the experience only when invited, rather than define the core system (Peng et al., 2023; Pasca et al., 2021).

Derived: R3, finding-balanceLeads to: DG4, decision-4
Design Decisions & Trade-Offs

Insight-to-Design Architectural Mapping

Every architectural move introduces both deliberate benefits and explicit trade-offs that were navigated during the project.

Move 01•Visitors want cultural meaning without losing immersion

Context-aware cultural interpretation layer

The system should reveal the meaning of landmarks through lightweight, location-aware storytelling that supports appreciation of the site rather than competing with it.

Acknowledged Trade-Off

Richer interpretation improves cultural understanding, but it requires careful curation so the experience remains calm and not overloaded.

Move 02•Audio is preferred on-site because it keeps attention on the place

Audio-first situated experience

On-site guidance should prioritize spoken narration and subtle cues, allowing users to keep their eyes on the scenery while still receiving historical and cultural context.

Acknowledged Trade-Off

Audio supports immersion better than text, but it must be paced carefully so it does not compete with ambient sound or feel intrusive.

Move 03•Planning support matters before the visit begins

Pre-visit insight and crowd awareness layer

Before arrival, the system should help visitors anticipate the experience through crowd forecasts, landmark previews, and best visiting windows, reducing uncertainty and supporting smoother decisions.

Acknowledged Trade-Off

Pre-visit tools improve confidence and comfort, but require more up-front content and reliable crowd information.

Move 04•Excessive phone interaction creates cognitive and experiential friction

Minimal visual interface with subtle navigation cues

Visual screens should avoid dense text or complex UI controls while walking; orientation cues and audio triggers keep visitors looking outward at the architecture.

Acknowledged Trade-Off

A minimal interface preserves physical immersion, but requires very clear, well-timed ambient audio cues so visitors do not feel lost.

Move 05•Deeper interaction should be optional, not the default

Optional narrative paths and cultural collectibles

Interactive story threads, collectible keepsakes, and AI-enhanced exploration should be available as opt-in layers for visitors who want extra depth, without disrupting those who prefer a quieter experience.

Acknowledged Trade-Off

Optional depth broadens appeal, but these features must be framed carefully so they feel culturally meaningful rather than overly gamified.

[09]Human-Centered Design Principles
设计原则 · 以人为本

Three Grounded Interaction Principles

Grounded in cognitive psychology and human-centered heritage interaction research.

Principle 01

Reduce cognitive load through audio-first interaction

In a dynamic outdoor heritage environment, constantly reading dense mobile typography and toggling apps overtaxes visual attention. Delivering contextual narratives through situated audio lets visitors keep their gaze on the stone canal, water reflections, and architecture.

HCI Anchor: Cognitive Load Theory & Ambient Computing
Principle 02

Support user control through when interaction happens

Visitors should never feel rushed or blindsided. By shifting spatial decisions and crowd assessments to the pre-visit planning phase, visitors arrive calm and oriented, preserving on-site tranquility for presence rather than logistics.

HCI Anchor: Temporal Decoupling & User Agency
Principle 03

Accommodate diverse preferences through optional interaction

Not all visitors seek the same depth. Some want meditative quiet; others desire historical debates and photo spots. Layering deep engagement as strictly opt-in ensures the default system remains serene, accommodating both personas seamlessly.

HCI Anchor: Layered Disclosure & Inclusive Experience
[10]Iterative Concept Evolution
概念演进 · 三阶迭代

Three Stages of Architectural Refinement

Tracing the design progression from initial feature-heavy brainstorming through interview-driven reduction to the final cultural companion.

Our design did not emerge fully formed; it evolved through three distinct iterations. Each shift was triggered directly by user insights that exposed cognitive overload and re-centered our focus on acoustic presence.

Iteration 1
01

Initial Concept: Feature-Heavy Tourist App

The first iteration explored a wide range of features through divergent thinking, including AI tour guides, interactive story quests, real-time crowd tracking, and reward-based systems. The design aimed to combine learning, navigation, and engagement into a single app experience, but quickly became overloaded and screen-dependent.

Refinement
02

Post-Interview Shift: Reducing Complexity

Interviews revealed that most visitors did not want a highly interactive or game-like experience. Instead, they valued cultural understanding, minimal phone use, and a more relaxed visit. Many preferred audio explanations over reading and expressed frustration with missing context due to language barriers. This led to a convergent design shift: removing heavy gamification, simplifying interaction, and focusing on when and how interaction should happen rather than adding more features.

Final Concept
03

Final Concept: Context-Aware Cultural Companion

The final design converges into a structured, three-phase experience: pre-visit planning, a lightweight on-site experience, and optional post-visit engagement. Instead of maximizing features, the system prioritizes reducing cognitive load, supporting user control, and enabling a more immersive experience through audio-first interaction. This reflects established HCI design principles, including reducing short-term memory load, maintaining visibility of system behaviour, and supporting user control through simplified interaction design.

[11]Final System Architecture & Experience Flow
最终方案 · 交互体验

Maple Bridge: The Three-Phase Cultural Companion

A mobile-optimized system structured across Before, During, and After visit moments.

Final System Proposition

A prototype of a context-aware cultural companion that supports visitors before, during, and after their visit through lightweight interaction and audio storytelling. The system focuses on helping visitors understand the cultural meaning of Maple Bridge without requiring constant screen interaction. Rather than relying on complex personalization, the design emphasizes simple exploration, optional depth, and minimal disruption to the on-site experience.

Research Prototype Scope & Crowd Simulation Disclosure

In strict adherence to academic transparency, the Maple Bridge application is an evaluative prototype. While interactive map navigation, audio playback, and bilingual content are fully operational, the crowd forecasting feature is implemented using simulated benchmark data to demonstrate interaction intent rather than live telemetry from production CCTV networks.

Primary Evaluative Discovery

The Demand for Structured, Actionable Crowd Foresight

While participants strongly approved of the calm, audio-first narrative companion, their most acute critique centered on the crowd preview. The Alpha prototype presented crowd density as a single generalized metric (e.g. “Moderate”). Participants argued this was too vague to inform real planning.

Observed User Feedback

“Knowing the whole scenic area is ‘moderate’ doesn’t tell me if the bridge arch itself is packed right now, or if tomorrow morning would be calmer.”

Iterative Design Outcome

Redesigned into a four-way structured forecast: Today, This Week, By Spot, and Upcoming Events affecting spatial flow.

Validated Qualitative Themes from Participants

Acoustic Heads-Up Presence

Participants confirmed that listening to short historical explanations while gazing at the water felt significantly more relaxing and culturally grounded than reading mobile articles on Baidu Baike or Dianping.

Relief from App-Switching

International participants highlighted that having English narration with original Tang poetic context eliminated the friction of running real-time camera translation tools over ancient stone inscriptions.

1. The Trigger

Alpha testing showed that users liked the idea of crowd information, but found the original display too general. They wanted to understand crowd levels by time, by specific spot, and by events that might affect their visit.

2. What Changed

The crowd feature was redesigned into a clearer forecast module. Instead of presenting crowd information as one broad status, the refined version separates it into Today, This Week, By Spot, and Upcoming Events. This makes the information easier to interpret before and during a visit.

3. The Outcome

The change made crowd information more actionable. Users can now decide when to visit, which landmark to prioritise, and whether an upcoming event may affect the atmosphere of a specific spot.

[16]Evaluation Reflection & Critical Analysis
反思与评估 · 经验沉淀

Critical Reflection on Process & Impact

Examining what succeeded, what failed, and how the research reshaped our understanding of cultural HCI.

Addressing Core Visitor Pain Points

Pain Point 01

Language friction and inaccessible signage

One of the clearest issues identified during research was that most on-site signage was only available in Chinese, which made it difficult for international visitors to understand the stories connected to the site. The final prototype addresses this through English–Mandarin interface content and English audio narration, reducing the need to rely entirely on external translation tools during exploration.

“During walkthrough sessions and informal feedback discussions, several participants said the English narration and bilingual written content made the site easier to understand and helped them connect landmarks to the poem and surrounding history. While this was not formally measured, participants generally appeared more engaged once contextual explanations were introduced.”
Pain Point 02

Excessive phone dependency

Research participants frequently described frustration with constantly checking their phones while visiting cultural sites. In response, the final prototype shifted toward a more audio-first interaction style, where information could be accessed without requiring continuous visual attention.

“Feedback during prototype walkthroughs suggested that participants preferred the lighter interaction style over more screen-heavy concepts explored earlier in the project. Several participants commented that the experience felt calmer and less distracting once audio became the primary interaction method.”
Pain Point 03

Lack of calm, meaningful reflection

Earlier interviews suggested that many visits felt visually memorable but culturally shallow, with visitors leaving without a strong understanding of what they had experienced. The final concept therefore combined lightweight pre-visit context, audio storytelling, and optional post-visit elements to encourage a more reflective experience.

“Informal post-walkthrough discussions suggested that participants responded positively to the calmer storytelling direction, particularly when cultural explanations were tied directly to landmarks and atmosphere rather than presented as large blocks of information. Several participants were also able to recall specific stories or historical details afterward.”
Methodological Takeaways

What This Project Taught Us

The Alpha test showed that users may like a feature in principle but still struggle with how the information is organised. The crowd feature was useful, but only became meaningful after it was broken down by time, location, and event context.
The project reinforced the importance of connecting requirements, prototype decisions, and evaluation evidence. The strongest design changes came from feedback that was specific enough to act on, such as the request for clearer crowd information by spot.
The switch to a more subtle approach as compared to overreliance on AI and gamified features proved to be more beneficial as most of the participants opted for more of a hands-free experience.
AI tools helped speed up prototyping and interface exploration, but the design direction still had to be judged against user feedback. Some AI-generated ideas looked polished but did not match the calm, low-distraction experience users wanted.
Opportunities for Evolution

Future Research Directions

Conduct a more structured usability test using task completion, time-on-task, and satisfaction ratings
Replace simulated crowd data with a clearly labelled real data source if the system is developed further
Improve accessibility by adding captions or transcript alternatives for all audio content
Continue testing the balance between cultural storytelling and screen-light interaction in an actual site visit

Failures

Design Influence

AI tools made it easier to explore very different directions early on, even though many of those ideas were later reduced or removed.

What Worked

AI tools were useful for generating alternative interaction ideas quickly, but many of the initial suggestions (e.g. gamified features, overly complex UI patterns) conflicted with user research. This required actively rejecting and filtering outputs rather than directly applying them.

Prompting for UI structure made it easier to prototype and revise presentation components in React.

Language-model support helped synthesize recurring themes across interview notes and persona drafts.

Image generation tools were used selectively to visualise early-stage ideas (e.g. rough app directions and interaction scenarios in Week 1). These were based on concepts we had already defined, and helped us externalise and compare directions quickly. They were not used to generate final designs, which were refined manually through iteration.

Limitations Observed

One recurring issue was that AI-generated ideas often looked convincing before being checked properly against the research. Some suggestions sounded reasonable on the surface but introduced interactions that users had already pushed back against, such as overly gamified features or unnecessary complexity. Because of this, generated outputs were treated more like starting points for discussion than final answers.
